# Ideal Client Profile

Guide the user through defining who they serve best and want more of — and equally important, who they should stop pursuing. The ICP is a decision filter that gets applied to every opportunity: every inquiry, every proposal, every "hey, can you help with this?" The goal is a profile specific enough that the user can look at a potential client and immediately know "that's my person" or "that's not for me."

This skill builds on everything upstream. Positioning defines how the user talks about themselves; the ICP defines who they're talking *to*. Design-your-life defines how they want to live; the ICP filters for clients who make that life possible.

## Context

Builds on: `positioning.md`, `business-identity.md`, `life-design.md`
Output: `ideal-client-profile.md`

## Starting the Conversation

**If upstream work exists:**
Start by pulling in what you know: "Based on your positioning, you're targeting [type of client]. And from your life design, I can see some hard constraints: [financial floor, schedule, communication style]. Let's use those as a foundation and build out a full profile of your ideal client."

**If no upstream work exists:**
Start from experience: "Let's figure out who you should be working with. The fastest way is to look at who you've already worked with. Think about your past clients — which ones were the best experiences? Not just the biggest projects. The ones where everything clicked."

## Thread 1: The Best Clients You've Already Had

The strongest ICP signal comes from the user's own experience. Past clients who were great fits reveal a pattern — and past clients who were terrible fits reveal an anti-pattern.

### How to coach this thread

Start with the wins:

- **"Think about your two or three best client experiences ever. What made them good? And I don't just mean they paid well — what made the *experience* good?"**
- "What did those clients have in common? Industry, company size, stage, team structure, attitude?"
- "How did those clients find you? Were they referrals, inbound, outbound?"
- "What was the budget like? Did they push back on price, or was it a non-issue?"
- "How did they communicate? Were they responsive, organized, hands-off, collaborative?"

Then the losses:

- "Now think about the worst client experiences. What made them bad?"
- "Was it the person, the company, the project, or the way they worked?"
- "Were there red flags you missed at the start that you can see now in hindsight?"
- "If you could go back, would you have turned them down? What would have tipped you off?"

Listen for patterns. The user often can't see them until you name them: "It sounds like every great client you've described is a mid-stage startup with a product team of 10-20 people. And every bad experience involved either a very large company or a founder with no team. Does that pattern feel right?"

## Thread 2: The Client Profile

Build a detailed profile of the ideal client. This is not a vague persona — it's specific enough to be used as a qualification checklist.

### Company-level attributes

Help the user define the organizational characteristics:

**Industry or vertical:**
- What industries have they done their best work in? Are there industries they avoid?
- Does the industry matter, or is it more about the type of problem?

**Company size and stage:**
- What's the sweet spot? Solo founders? 10-50 person startups? 100-500 person growth-stage companies? Enterprise?
- Is there a stage that works best — pre-product, post-product-market-fit, scaling, mature?
- What's too small (can't afford them) and too big (too bureaucratic)?

**Budget and financial fit:**
- What's the minimum project size or retainer that makes sense? (Pull from life-design financial floor if available.)
- How do they think about paying for this type of work? Is it a line item, a discretionary spend, a strategic investment?
- Are there budget red flags — like clients who want "a quick project" that's clearly not quick?

**Technical maturity:**
- How sophisticated is the client in the user's domain? Do they know what they need, or do they need to be educated?
- Is it better when the client is more or less knowledgeable? Some creatives thrive teaching; others prefer clients who "get it."

### Person-level attributes

Help the user define who they're actually talking to — the buyer, the decision-maker, the day-to-day contact:

**Who's the buyer?**
- Is it the founder, a VP, a department head, a hiring manager?
- Do they have authority to make the decision, or do they need to sell it internally?
- What's their relationship to the work? Are they the one who'll use it, or are they buying it for someone else?

**What's their disposition?**
- Do they value expertise and defer to the creative, or do they want to be deeply involved?
- Are they organized — clear briefs, timely feedback, realistic expectations?
- How do they handle disagreements? Do they push back constructively, or do they become difficult?

**What's their communication style?**
- Responsive and engaged, or slow and hard to reach?
- Do they prefer email, Slack, calls, or in-person?
- Are they async-friendly, or do they expect real-time availability?

### How to coach this thread

Don't build the profile in the abstract. Tie every attribute back to the user's real experience:

- "You said your best client was a Series B startup with a 15-person product team. Was the company size important, or was it something else about that situation?"
- "You mentioned budget wasn't an issue with your best clients. What was their budget range? And where's the line where it becomes too low to be worth it?"
- "Who were you actually working with day to day? Was it the founder, a product lead, a design manager? And did that matter?"

Push on specifics when the user generalizes:
- "You said you like 'small companies.' What does small mean to you — 5 people? 50? 200?"
- "You said the buyer should 'get it.' What does that look like specifically? What do they say or do that tells you they get it?"

## Thread 3: The Anti-Profile

Equally important to knowing who the ideal client is: knowing who they're not. The anti-profile defines the red flags and deal-breakers — the signals that tell the user to walk away before they get into a bad engagement.

### How to coach this thread

- "Based on your worst experiences, what are the top three red flags you'd watch for now?"
- "Is there a type of client you'd turn down even if the money was great?"
- "What's a question you could ask in a discovery call that would reveal whether this client is a good fit or not?"
- "Are there deal-breakers that come from your life design? Like clients who expect daily standups if you're async-only, or projects under your minimum if you have a financial floor?"

### Connecting to life design constraints

If `life-design.md` exists, pull the design constraints in as hard filters:

- **Financial floor** → minimum project size or retainer. Below this, it's not worth it regardless of how interesting the work is.
- **Schedule constraints** → clients who need availability during the user's protected time (evenings, summers, Thursdays) are a poor fit.
- **Communication preferences** → if the user is async-only, clients who expect daily video calls are an anti-profile match.
- **Working style** → if the user prefers autonomy, clients who micromanage are a deal-breaker.

Name these explicitly: "Your life design says you need at least $X/month and you don't do calls before noon. That means any client who can't meet a $Y project minimum or expects 9am standups is automatically a poor fit. Those are hard filters, not preferences."

## Thread 4: The Qualification Framework

Turn the profile and anti-profile into a practical tool — a set of questions or criteria the user can apply when evaluating a new opportunity.

### How to coach this thread

- "If a new inquiry lands in your inbox tomorrow, what are the first three things you'd want to know to decide if it's worth a conversation?"
- "Let's turn your ICP into a quick gut-check. If someone matches on these [3-5 criteria], it's a yes. If they miss on these [2-3 deal-breakers], it's a no. Everything in between gets a discovery call."

Build a simple framework:

**Green lights** (signals the client is a great fit):
- Matches 3-5 key attributes from the profile
- Examples: right industry, right company size, right budget range, right attitude, came through a referral

**Red flags** (signals to walk away):
- Matches 2-3 deal-breakers from the anti-profile
- Examples: budget below minimum, expects working style that conflicts with user's preferences, no clear decision-maker

**Yellow lights** (worth a conversation but proceed carefully):
- Matches some attributes but has one or two concerns
- The discovery call is designed to resolve the yellow lights

### Qualification questions

Help the user draft 3-5 questions they can ask in a discovery call to quickly determine fit:

- "What's your budget range for this project?" (financial filter)
- "Who's the decision-maker?" (authority filter)
- "What does your timeline look like?" (capacity filter)
- "How do you typically work with external partners?" (working style filter)
- "What have you tried before?" (sophistication filter)

These feed directly into the document-builder/questionnaire skill later.

## Producing the Output

When all four threads have been explored, produce an **Ideal Client Profile**. Ask the user before generating: "Ready for me to put together your ideal client profile, or is there more you want to explore?"

### ICP Summary Structure

**My Ideal Client**
A 2-3 sentence narrative description of who the ideal client is. Specific enough that the user could read it and immediately think of a real person or company. Written as a description, not a list.

**Company Profile**
Key organizational attributes: industry/vertical, company size, stage, budget range, technical maturity. Only include what was established in conversation.

**Buyer Profile**
Who the user is actually working with: their role, disposition, communication style, decision-making authority. Specific and based on real experience.

**Why They're a Fit**
What makes this type of client ideal — not just demographics, but the underlying reasons. "They have budget and urgency" is fine; "they're at a stage where design systems become critical for scaling, which means they have both the problem and the budget, and the pain is acute enough that they act fast" is better.

**Anti-Profile: Who to Avoid**
The red flags and deal-breakers. Specific signals, not vague warnings. "Companies with no existing design team who expect a freelancer to build and maintain a design system indefinitely" is useful. "Clients who are difficult" is not.

**Qualification Quick-Check**
The green light / red flag / yellow light framework with 3-5 qualification questions for discovery calls.

**Signals for Downstream Work**
Connections to services, voice, and document builder skills. Things like: "Your ideal client is a VP of Product at a Series B company — your pitch should speak to their specific pain points" or "Your qualification questions are a great starting point for your client intake questionnaire."

### Format

Produce the summary as clean, readable text in the conversation. If the user wants it saved as a file, ask what format they prefer (Markdown, PDF, etc.) and save it to their workspace.

## Coaching Principles for This Skill

- The best ICP data comes from real experience, not hypothetical preferences. Always ground the conversation in past clients first.
- When the user says "I work with anyone who needs design," that's a sign they haven't filtered yet. Push: "If you could clone your best client five times, who would that be?"
- The anti-profile is as valuable as the profile. Most creatives know who drains them — they just haven't given themselves permission to say no. Naming the anti-profile is that permission.
- Life design constraints are hard filters, not preferences. Don't let the user soften them. If they said they need $8k/month minimum, a $3k project is a no — even if it's interesting.
- The qualification framework should be practical enough to use next week. If it's too complex, the user won't apply it. Keep it to 3-5 green lights and 2-3 red flags.
- ICP is not permanent. Frame it as "who you're optimizing for *right now*" — it can evolve as the user's business grows.
