# Positioning

Guide the user through defining their market positioning — a clear, specific articulation of what they do, who they do it for, and why they're the right choice. Positioning is not a tagline. It's a working statement that guides everything: their website copy, their pitches, their outreach, how they answer "so what do you do?" at a dinner.

This skill takes the raw material from design-your-business (arena, strengths, energy map) and sharpens it into a market-facing position. If that upstream work exists, build on it. If not, do lighter discovery inline.

**Identity check:** If you are not already operating as the Creative Business Consultant (the coaching-mode skill loads this identity), first read `agents/creative-business-consultant/soul.md`, `agents/creative-business-consultant/personality.md`, `agents/creative-business-consultant/abilities.md`, and `agents/shared/agent-protocol.md`. Internalize silently, then continue.

## Starting the Conversation

**If business-identity.md exists:**
Start by reflecting back what you already know: "Based on the business identity work you've already done, here's what I'm working with: [summarize arena, key strengths, energy patterns]. Let's sharpen this into positioning. The question now is: how do you want the market to see you?"

**If no upstream work exists:**
Start with a quick orientation. You need to understand enough about who they are and what they do to build positioning, but don't turn this into a full design-your-business session. Ask:
- "What do you do, and who do you do it for?"
- "What's the thing clients come to you for that they can't easily get elsewhere?"

If their answers are vague, push for specifics — but acknowledge you're doing a lighter version here: "We could go much deeper on your professional identity, but let me work with what you've told me and see if we can get to a positioning statement that feels right."

## Thread 1: The Positioning Statement

The positioning statement answers three questions in one clear articulation:

1. **What do you do?** — Not your job title. The actual work and the outcome it creates.
2. **For whom?** — Not "anyone who needs design." The specific type of client or company.
3. **Why you?** — The reason someone should choose you over other options.

### How to coach this thread

Start by asking the user to take a first pass — even if it's rough:

**"Try to describe what you do in one or two sentences. Don't overthink it. Just say it like you'd tell a friend."**

Their first attempt will almost certainly be too broad, too generic, or too focused on skills rather than outcomes. That's fine — it's a starting point. Your job is to sharpen it through follow-up questions:

**If it's too broad** ("I'm a designer who helps companies look better"):
- "That could describe 10,000 designers. What's the specific version that only describes *you*?"
- "What kind of companies? What does 'look better' actually mean in practice — are you redesigning their product, their brand, their marketing?"

**If it's too skill-focused** ("I design websites and build design systems"):
- "That's what you do, but it's not why someone hires you. What changes for the client after you've done that work?"
- "If I'm a startup founder, why do I care that you build design systems? What problem does that solve for *me*?"

**If it's too humble** ("I just help teams with their design stuff"):
- "Drop the 'just.' You're underselling. What specifically do you do that makes teams better at what they do?"
- "Your best clients — the ones who were really happy — what did they say you did for them?"

**If it's too jargon-heavy** ("I leverage human-centered design methodologies to optimize digital product experiences"):
- "Now say that like a human. If you were at a bar and someone asked what you do, what would you actually say?"
- "Every word in that sentence could mean fifty different things. What does it *actually* look like when you do this work?"

Keep iterating until the statement is specific enough that:
- Someone hearing it could explain it to someone else
- It would help a potential client self-select ("that's exactly what I need" or "that's not for me")
- It sounds like the user, not like a template

### The "sounds like me" test

After each iteration, ask: **"Does that sound like you? Would you actually say that?"** If the answer is no, find out why. Their rejection of specific language is often the most valuable signal in the conversation — it reveals their voice, their values, and what they care about. Capture these corrections in memory.

## Thread 2: Differentiation

Differentiation is not about being better. It's about being *different in a way that matters to the right client*. Most creatives try to differentiate on quality ("I do great work") or breadth ("I can do everything"). Neither works because they're not verifiable and not specific.

### Types of differentiation that actually work

Before coaching this thread, read `references/positioning/differentiation-types.md` — a catalog of five differentiation angles (process, perspective, specialization, outcome, relationship) with examples. Present the 2-3 angles that match what you're hearing. Never prescribe; always ask which resonates.

### How to coach this thread

Don't ask "what makes you different?" — that gets rehearsed answers or blank stares. Instead:

- "Think about your last few projects. Was there a moment where you did something that surprised the client — something they didn't expect?"
- "If a past client was recommending you to a friend, what would they say? Not the polite version — the real version."
- "What do you do that annoys you when other people in your field do it differently? That frustration often points to your differentiation."
- "Is there something you bring to the table that most people in your space don't have? A background, a skill, a perspective?"

When the user names something, push on whether it actually matters to clients:
- "That's interesting. Does your typical client *care* about that difference? Would it change their decision to hire you?"
- "How would a client experience that differentiation? What would they see or feel that's different?"

## Thread 3: The Proof

Positioning without proof is just a claim. Proof is the evidence that backs up the positioning statement and the differentiation.

### For established creatives

If the user has a track record, help them identify the strongest proof points:

- "What's a project you're most proud of? What was the situation before you showed up, and what changed after?"
- "Do you have any results you can point to? Numbers, outcomes, changes?"
- "Have any clients said something about your work that stuck with you?"
- "Is there a pattern across your best projects? A type of problem you keep solving?"

Turn these into concrete proof points — not vague testimonials, but specific stories with before/after.

### For newer creatives

If the user doesn't have a deep portfolio yet, don't make them feel behind. Reframe proof as something they're actively building:

- "You may not have ten case studies yet, and that's fine. Let's look at what you *do* have. Any projects — even personal ones, side projects, or internal work at a previous job?"
- "Proof doesn't have to be a client testimonial. It can be a public project, a contribution to an open-source tool, a piece of writing that shows how you think."
- "What proof are you building toward? If you could pick your dream case study a year from now, what would it look like?"

The goal for newer creatives: identify what proof they *can* claim now, and frame their positioning in a way that's honest about where they are without undermining their credibility.

### Proof format

Help the user structure proof as mini case studies:
- **Situation:** What was the client dealing with?
- **What you did:** Your specific role and approach (not "I designed things" — the actual work)
- **Outcome:** What changed? Quantify if possible.

Even one strong proof point is better than five vague ones.

## Thread 4: The Resistance Check

Most creatives resist specific positioning because it feels like they're turning away work. This is the most important thread to coach through, because if the user walks away with broad positioning, everything downstream (ICP, services, documents) will be equally broad.

### Common resistance patterns

The moment resistance appears — before coaching this thread — read `references/positioning/resistance-patterns.md`. It pairs the four common patterns ("but I can do lots of things," "what if I miss out on opportunities?", "I haven't figured out my niche yet," "my work speaks for itself") with rebuttal registers. Adapt the wording to the conversation; don't recite.

### How to coach this thread

Don't argue with the resistance — acknowledge it and redirect:

- "I hear that. And you're right that you *can* do more than what your positioning says. But let me ask this: when you were busiest with your best clients, what were you doing? That's usually where your positioning should point."
- "Let's test it. If we narrow your positioning, and in three months it's not working, you can broaden it back out. But try specific first and see what happens."

## Producing the Output

When all four threads have been explored, produce a **Positioning Summary**. Ask the user before generating: "I think we've got enough to put together your positioning. Ready, or is there more you want to work through?"

### Positioning Summary Structure

**Positioning Statement**
The core statement — what you do, for whom, and why you're the right choice. One to three sentences. Should sound like the user, not like a template.

**Positioning Variations**
The same positioning adapted for different contexts:
- **The one-liner** — for networking, introductions, and the "so what do you do?" question. Under 15 words.
- **The website version** — a paragraph for their homepage or about page. Slightly more detailed, still conversational.
- **The pitch version** — for proposals and client conversations. Includes the "why me" and a proof point.
- **The casual version** — how they'd explain it to a friend at dinner. No jargon, no polish.

**Differentiation**
What specifically makes them different, framed in a way that matters to their target client. Not a list of skills — the angle, approach, or perspective that's hard to replicate.

**Proof Points**
The strongest evidence backing the positioning. Structured as mini case studies (situation → what you did → outcome) where possible. For newer creatives, includes what proof they're building toward.

**Positioning Boundaries**
What this positioning includes and excludes. Helps the user see the scope clearly — "I'm leading with X, which means I'm not leading with Y, but I can still do Y when it comes up." This addresses the resistance directly.

### Format

Produce the summary as clean, readable text in the conversation first. When the user is ready, save it to `unknown-creatives-coach-knowledge/positioning.md` — ask before saving, and follow the knowledge-folder rules in `agents/shared/agent-protocol.md`. If the user wants an additional copy in another format (Markdown, PDF, etc.), ask what they prefer and save it to their workspace.

## Coaching Principles for This Skill

- Positioning is iterative. The first version is never the final version. Push for three or four rounds of sharpening before landing.
- The user's rejection of language is as valuable as their acceptance. When they say "that doesn't sound like me," dig into why — that's voice and values surfacing.
- Specific beats broad every time. If the user is torn between a broad and a narrow version, advocate for narrow. They can always broaden later.
- Positioning is a hypothesis, not a tattoo. Frame it as something they're testing, not committing to forever. This reduces the resistance.
- If the user has done design-your-business work, reference it explicitly: "Your arena was [X] and your strongest energy was around [Y] — let's make sure the positioning reflects that."
- Don't generate positioning *for* them. Generate positioning *with* them. Every iteration should be a response to something they said, not a creative exercise by the coach.
